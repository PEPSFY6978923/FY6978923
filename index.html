Abstract

"This paper evaluates to what extent jet engines ended the Falklands War. It investigates how the supply of engines nulled the Argentinian Airforce and how the British gained air superiority. This is measured by looking at the cost to both sides from each attack, the political influence on other countries, the major networks destroyed and the aftermath of the war as a direct result from the Airforce. The main argument for both sides is the armament on both aircraft and ground – generally Argentina were equipped with more aircraft and ground to air attacks, however the British had much more complex aircraft providing a ratio of 20:1 loss of aircraft from the Argentinians to British."
Introduction
"On April 2nd, 1982, the Argentinians attacked the British owned Falkland Islands under the rule of the dictatorship known as Proceso de Reorganización Nacional. They seized South Georgia. The British responded by deploying the RAF among over British Forces over the Falklands until the 16th of June. The British claimed air superiority due to many different factors, which primarily consisted of Britain’s superior control. E.g. they could land the Sea Harrier (Jump jet) on rough terrain to land whereas Argentinian jets required 700 metres of smooth flat ground. These include the Rolls-Royce Pegasus 104/3 and the Rolls-Royce Olympus 301 (later used in the Concorde to achieve supersonic flight from London to New York in just 3 hours and 30 minutes compared to the current ~8 hours). This article intends to explore the depths of the British and Argentinian jet engines in how they effected speed, maintance, control and supply and the influence on ending the war. "
